The shape card: nine drawings of one number, and what each costs

The round scores five measures - pace, time to start, longest gap, ums a minute, longest sentence - and four of them were comfortable. That is the whole content of this card. The radar draws it as a pentagon with no labels and no key, which is why it reads as a blob; and every one of the five already has its own card two inches away, so the only thing this card adds is the count and the comparison with your usual.

Click a card in the gallery to put it on the board. Every drawing below is a shape people have seen before: a bar chart, a pie with the slices pulled apart, a dial, a progress bar.

The bar family

  • Columns. Five bars, one a measure, full height where the round was comfortable and short where it was not. The plainest chart there is, and it plots exactly the numbers the radar plots - a radar is a bar chart bent into a circle, so this is that circle cut open and laid flat. The short bar is the thing that went wrong and it says its own name underneath, which no other drawing here does without a sentence.
  • Bars, one a measure. The same, on its side. Turning it is what lets the names be words rather than abbreviations: "Longest sentence" fits at the left of a row and does not fit under a column.
  • Segmented bar. One track cut into five blocks. Not a measurement at all, a tally: four full and one empty, the way a progress bar reads.
  • The cost these three share: bar height is a score, not a number you can quote. A short Pace bar does not mean 111 is a small number, it means 111 fell outside the comfortable stretch. Somebody skimming can misread that, and the caption has to carry it.

The pie family

  • Broken pie. A circle cut into five slices with gaps between them, four accent and one warm, the count in the middle. Equal slices are normally a mistake in a pie; here they are the point, because the five measures weigh the same and the only question is how many were comfortable. A fifth of a ring in another colour is a shape you read before you read a word.
  • Arc gauge. The same pie folded to a half circle, which is what a dial is. Sits better in a wide short card and leaves more room under it for the sentence.
  • Ring. One unbroken donut, four fifths accent, count in the middle. The simplest of all, with nothing to count: a ring that is nearly whole.
  • The cost the pie family shares: where a slice sits means nothing. The warm slice is warm because pace was outside, but its position on the circle is arbitrary, and it cannot name what went wrong - the sentence under the card has to.

Not charts

  • Named marks. A big "4 of 5 comfortable" and five little pills, ticked or crossed. The card then reads exactly like every other card on the board: a number, a word, then the detail.
  • Two numbers. No drawing at all: "4 of 5" against "your usual 3 of 5", side by side, the way the streak tiles already read. The least this card could possibly be.
  • No card. The count moves into the head row and the board goes to nine bigger cards, which on a filmed screen may be worth more than a tenth box.

The round page is a different room

  • The same radar is on /streak/[id], labelled and keyed and drawn beside the very bars it is a picture of, so four measures appear twice on one line: once as a spike with no number and once as a bar with one.
  • Named zones keeps the bars and fixes what actually makes them hard: the three stretches are drawn along the track and the one you landed in is named under it - "slow", "comfortable", "rushed" - instead of two words at the far ends. Your usual stays a hollow dot.
  • Bars, one a measure is the column chart at page size, in place of the radar, with each measure's real number at the end of its row.
  • With your last ten puts a small line of your last ten rounds on each row. The only option that answers "am I getting better", and the only one with a cost behind it: usual is a mean over twelve rounds and not a series, so the backend would have to send ten points a measure.
The recommendation: columns on the board, named zones on the round page. The bar chart is the only drawing here that carries both halves of the message on its own - how many were comfortable, and which one was not - without anybody reading a sentence, and a short bar among tall ones is as legible across a room as a gap in a ring. The pie family is prettier and says half as much: a ring cannot name what went wrong. If the card should be beautiful before it is informative, take the broken pie; if it should be neither, take the two numbers, which is the honest floor. On the round page there is no picture worth drawing, only bars that finally explain themselves.
